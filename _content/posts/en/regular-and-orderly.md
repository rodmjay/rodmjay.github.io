The Flaubert line in my X bio is the closest thing I have to a work philosophy: "Be regular and orderly in your life, so that you may be violent and original in your work." He wrote *Madame Bovary* that way, on a strict daily routine.

Early in my career I kept everything in my head. Deadlines, follow-ups, whatever I'd promised somebody in a hallway. Most weeks that worked. Then a bad one would come along, something would slip, and I'd spend the next week cleaning it up.

It wasn't an effort problem. Memory is a lousy database. It fails under load, and load is exactly when you need it.

So I quit asking it to do that job. If I'd have to remember something twice, I write it down or automate it. Bills are on autopay and recurring work is on the calendar. Anything open lives in one place, and I look at it on a schedule instead of when I get anxious.

It's dull. That's fine. Once the ordinary stuff runs on rails, it stops fighting for my attention. Mornings start the same way and most weeks look alike. Saturday I look back at the week and pick what's next. Sunday I rest, and I keep it that way.

So when a hard design problem lands, say a system that has to survive ten years of change, I can give it all my attention. I'm not also wondering if the electric bill got paid.

## Same thing at work

At some point I noticed I run architecture the same way. A good platform makes the common path boring. Logging works the same in every service and deploys all look alike, so nobody spends a thought on them. Teams don't waste their good ideas on plumbing. They save them for the actual product.

I've watched teams go the other way, where every service rolls its own logging and every project starts from an empty folder. It feels like freedom for about six months. After that it's why nothing ships. Everyone's re-deciding things that should've been decided once.

If you want to try this, a few things helped me. Keep tasks in one place and dates in another; once something lives in two places you stop trusting both. Look at it on a schedule, Saturday morning for me, rather than when you get nervous. And with recurring work, do it by hand the first time. The second time, write it down. If there's a third, automate it.
